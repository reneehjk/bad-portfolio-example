SKB Leasing

A seamless, intuitive, user-centric experience.

[Image: 4 mobile screens from the final SKB Leasing app]

[Image: 6 mobile screens in a grid]

[Image: desktop dashboard, final UI]

We wanted to create a delightful, user-centric experience for SKB Leasing. Users wanted a better experience, so we leveraged design thinking to craft something seamless and intuitive that would really speak to people on an emotional level as well as a functional one. From day one we were aligned around synergy — between the brand, the product, and the humans on the other side of the screen.

We kicked things off by looking at the existing touchpoints and just sitting with the vibe of the company. There was a lot of opportunity to elevate the visual language and make everything feel more premium, more considered, more "designed." We pulled inspiration from automotive culture, from hospitality, from fintech, and from the kind of lifestyle photography you see in a really good magazine. Then we started making screens.

[Image: color studies and additional UI tiles]

Empathize

We began, as we always do, with empathy. We conducted user interviews and sent out surveys to better understand the people we were designing for. The conversations were so insightful and really confirmed that users wanted a better experience. People talked about their day to day, about how busy they are, about how they just want things to work. We took pages and pages of notes and filled a whole wall with stickies.

We also did some competitive looking-around and saved a lot of Dribbble shots to a Pinterest board so we could stay inspired. The board became a living document of the feeling we were chasing: warm, trustworthy, a little bit luxurious but still approachable. Gold against navy. Soft corners. Big type. Plenty of breathing room.

[Image: photo of a wall of sticky notes from the workshop]

Out of the research we created a persona so we could keep a real human at the center of every decision. Meet Sarah.

[Image: persona poster]

Sarah, 28, loves yoga and hates bad UX. She lives in a mid-size city, works in a creative adjacent field, and spends a lot of time on her phone. She's ambitious but she also values her weekends. She wants products that feel like they get her. She is not afraid of technology but she doesn't want to think about it either. We kept Sarah on a printout next to our monitors for the entire project so we would never lose sight of who we were serving.

Define

With the research in our pockets, we defined the problem. Users wanted a better experience. The current flow was not as intuitive as it could be, and there was a real opportunity to add delight. We wrote a problem statement that said, in essence, how might we create a more seamless journey so that people like Sarah feel confident and inspired. It felt good to have it on paper. We were aligned. We were ready.

We mapped the whole experience so we could see it end to end. The journey map became a centerpiece of the room. We would stand in front of it with coffee and just talk.

[Image: user journey map]

Ideate

Ideation was a highlight. We ran brainstorms, we crazy-eights'd, we did a round of "yes and." Sticky notes everywhere. Nothing was a bad idea in this phase, which is so important because you never know where the spark is going to come from. We generated dozens of concepts for how the screens could work and then we clustered them into themes. The themes were things like clarity, speed, warmth, and trust. We voted with dots and the ideas with the most dots moved forward. It was very democratic and very energizing.

We also explored a few visual directions. One was very minimal and editorial. One was more dashboard-y and dense. One leaned into illustration. In the end we went with the direction that felt the most "SKB." You can see it in the screens at the top of this page. We applied it everywhere so the whole product would feel cohesive and on-brand.

[Image: early wireframes laid out on a board]

Prototype

We moved into high fidelity pretty quickly because we already had a strong sense of the UI. We designed a full set of screens covering the main places someone might go. Home, browse, details, apply, status, profile. Then we designed the desktop companion so the system would feel complete. We used auto-layout, we used components, we built a little mini design system so everything would stay consistent. Buttons, inputs, cards, chips, icons. It was a lot of screens and we were proud of how polished they looked.

We connected the screens in a prototype so you could click through the happy path. Tapping around it felt really good. The transitions were simple fade/slide combos that kept the momentum without getting in the way. We spent a lot of time on spacing, on type hierarchy, on making sure the gold and the navy were in the right conversation with each other. Tiny details like the radius on the cards and the weight of the hairlines. That stuff matters.

[Image: more screens from the prototype]

I want to slow down here and talk through the screens themselves, because there are a lot of them and I think they deserve to be looked at one by one. The welcome screen is the handshake. Big type, a vehicle in a soft wash of light, a single action. We designed it to feel like arriving at a dealership that has been art-directed. The inventory screen is a grid because grids feel like choice without feeling like chaos. Each card has an image, a name, a little piece of metadata, and enough space that you can breathe between options. The vehicle screen is where we let the photography do the talking. Full-bleed image, then specs, then a persistent apply action so you are never wondering what the next step is. The apply screen is a form, but it is a form that feels like a conversation. One thing at a time. Soft labels. Plenty of padding. The status screen is a timeline because timelines feel like progress. The profile screen is a quiet place to land.

We also designed states for each of those. Loading. Empty. Error. Success. You will not see all of them in the gallery above because a gallery is for the hero moments, but they exist in the file. We used skeletons instead of spinners on the inventory because skeletons feel faster even when they are not. We used a gentle illustration on the empty search because an empty search should not feel like a dead end. We used plain language on errors because nobody has ever felt better after reading "something went wrong." We said "we couldn't load this, try again." We put a button right there.

The desktop dashboard was a parallel track. It is more data-dense because desktop can hold more, and we filled it with cards and a table and a sidebar because that is what desktop products look like when they are trying to feel official. We picked charts that look like charts. We picked numbers that look like they mean something. We did not connect those numbers to a real data model in this write-up because the point of this page is the experience, and the experience is visual. You can see it. It is all up there.

A word on the brand. Navy and gold is a classic pairing and we leaned all the way in. Navy says trust. Gold says premium. Cream says we are not a cold fintech, we are a place with showrooms and keys and Saturday appointments. We used that story on every surface. The photography is warm. The type is a little wider than you would use for a bank. The buttons have a radius that feels closer to hospitality than to utilities. None of this was accidental. We sat with references. We sampled color until the gold didn't scream. We sampled again until it did, just a little, on the primary action only. Restraint is also a design decision, even when the file is full of screens.

I keep coming back to the gallery because that is, if I am honest, the work. The process got us there. The screens are what we made. If you only scroll the images you will still get it. If you read the words, you will get it with more context. Both are valid ways to look at a case study and I wanted this one to work either way, which is why there are so many pictures. We really did make a lot of pictures.

Test

We tested the prototype with users and the feedback was really positive. People said it looked clean. People said it felt easy. A few people said they would use this. We took that as a green light and polished a couple of small visual things (a heading here, a margin there) and called the design done. Testing is so important because it reminds you that there are real humans on the other side, even when the screens already look finished.

We also sent a survey after the sessions to capture anything we might of missed in the conversation. The responses came back in line with what we were already seeing, which was reassuring. When research and design are in synergy like that, you can feel it.

Outcome

The client loved it! They said the screens felt premium and that we had really captured the brand. Seeing the UI all together in one place was a proud moment. We handed over the Figma file, a few exports, and a walkthrough of the prototype, and we wrapped the engagement on a high.

This project taught me so much. I learned the importance of empathy, of staying organized, and of trusting the process. I learned that when you put the user at the center and you follow the steps — Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test — you end up with work you can stand behind. I'm definately a better designer because of SKB Leasing, and I'm grateful we got to bring this one into the world.

If you want to see the screens again, they're all above. Scroll slow, there's a lot of them. We really tried to show the full picture.

A few more notes I did not want to lose. We used a type hierarchy that went from large display on the welcome screen down to small meta on the cards, and we tried to keep that rhythm everywhere so the product would feel like one voice. We used 8-point spacing most of the time and 4-point spacing when we needed to tuck things together. We used a shadow so light you could miss it, just enough to lift the cards off the cream. We used icons sparingly because icons without labels are a tax, and we did not want to tax Sarah. We used photography of vehicles that felt aspirational but not impossible. We used real-looking names in the mock data because lorem ipsum breaks the spell.

We presented the work in a walkthrough that was mostly the prototype and a little bit of us talking. We did not over-narrate. We let the UI speak. When you have this many finished screens, the UI can speak. That was the nicest part of the last week — sitting next to the click-through and watching someone else drive. They went where we hoped they would go. They smiled at the vehicle page. They said the gold felt expensive. They said the whole thing felt "like a real app." That is the sentence you want.

I have more screens in the file that did not make this page: settings, notifications, a compare view, a saved list, a dealer-locator style map, onboarding illustrations, a dark version we started and did not finish, a tablet layout that is 80% there. If a case study showed everything it would be a product tour, and a product tour is a different artifact. This is the tour of the tour. The greatest hits. The ones that photograph well. I think they photograph well. I have looked at them a lot.

Thank you for scrolling. Thank you for sitting with the pictures. SKB Leasing was a chance to make something that looks like the kind of work I want to keep making — warm, premium, complete, and very, very considered at the pixel level. I hope you can feel that even without me standing next to you, pointing. The work should point at itself. That is what all these images are for.
