Project Phoenix

Reimagining a digital experience through design thinking, synergy, and a whole lot of heart.

Project Phoenix was one of those engagements where you can feel, from the first kickoff, that something special is going to happen. We were asked to come in and help make the experience better. That was the brief, more or less, and we took it seriously. Users wanted a better experience. Stakeholders wanted a better experience. We wanted to craft something seamless, intuitive, and genuinely delightful, and we were ready to leverage every part of the process to get there.

I'm not going to bury the lead: the client loved it. But I think the more interesting story is how we got there, because we followed a proven framework — Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test — and we followed it in order, with intention, and with a lot of sticky notes. What follows is a full write-up of that journey. Grab a coffee. This one is a long read on purpose. I wanted to document everything so that future-me, and anyone else who finds there way here, can see how a real project actually unfolds when you put people first.

We came into the room without assumptions, which is always my favorite way to start. There was a product. There were users. There was a sense that the current version of things had not quite lived up to its potential. Beyond that we gave ourselves permission not to know yet. Not-knowing is a design skill. It keeps you curious. It keeps you from jumping to screens before you have earned the screens. We set up a shared workspace, we named the channel, we picked a standing meeting, and we got to work.

A note on how we talk about this: when I say "we," I mean the project. Design is a team sport and I don't think it helps anyone to carve up credit. We workshopped, we synthesized, we presented, we revised. The work is the work. If you want to know who moved which rectangle, I'm not sure that's the point. The point is the experience we shipped and the process we honored along the way.

Empathize

Empathy is the foundation of everything I believe about design, so we started there and we stayed there. We conducted user interviews. We sent out surveys. We sat with the words people used and we tried to hear the need underneath the words. This phase is not glamorous and it shouldn't be. It is slow on purpose. It is about building a relationship with a problem before you try to solve it.

The interviews were wide ranging in the best way. People told us about their mornings, about the tools they already use, about the little workarounds they have invented because the official way of doing something didn't feel natural. They told us they want things to be easier. They told us they want to feel confident. They told us they don't want to think too hard. None of that was surprising, and that was actually the insight — users wanted a better experience, and they had been wanting it for a while. Hearing it out loud, in their own voices, made it real.

We did not go into those conversations with a rigid script because we wanted them to feel like conversations. We had a few prompts on a page. Tell us about the last time this felt hard. Tell us about the last time this felt easy. If you could change one thing, what would it be. Then we listened. Really listened. There is a difference between waiting for your turn to talk and actually receiving what someone is offering you. We tried to do the second one. We nodded a lot. We said "that's interesting" a lot. We wrote everything down.

The surveys ran in parallel so we could complement the qualitative with a little bit of the quantitative, which stakeholders always appreciate. We asked people to rate their current experience, to tell us how often they used various parts of the product, to share what would make them more likely to recommend it. The responses came back over a couple of weeks and we dropped them into a spreadsheet and just sat with the columns. There were patterns, the way there are always patterns. People wanted clarity. People wanted speed. People wanted to feel like the product was on their side. We highlighted cells. We made a chart. We put the chart in a deck.

Synthesis is where empathy becomes something you can point at. We printed quotes. We cut them up. We clustered. We argued, gently, about which cluster was actually two clusters. We gave the clusters names that sounded like they meant something: "need for reassurance," "too many steps," "want to feel smart," "don't make me look it up." Those names lived on the wall for the rest of the project. People would walk by and tap a cluster with their knuckle like it was a good luck charm. That is when you know the research has landed — when it becomes part of the furniture.

We also spent time just using the current experience ourselves, which I think is underrated. We clicked through. We got lost. We got un-lost. We took screenshots and annotated them with feelings rather than with solutions, because solutions come later. Feelings first. "Anxious here." "Bored here." "Proud here, weirdly." Those annotations became a second layer on top of the interview wall. Between the two we started to feel like we knew the texture of the problem even if we still couldn't have pitched it in a single sentence, which is fine, because that sentence belongs to Define.

Competitive empathy was part of this too. We looked at other products in adjacent spaces not to copy them but to understand what users in this world already expect. We made a matrix. We put logos along the top and attributes down the side. We colored in cells. It looked like a lot of work and it was a lot of work, and at the end of it we could say, with a straight face, that we had done our homework. Homework matters. It builds trust in the room. It lets you say "we looked" when someone asks if you looked.

I want to pause and talk about the emotional labor of this phase because I don't think we talk about it enough. Empathizing is tiring. You take in other people's frustrations and you hold them without immediately discharging them into a mockup. You go home and you still have someone's quote in your head while you're making dinner. That is the job. If you are not a little bit haunted by the research, you probably didn't get close enough. We were haunted in a productive way. We cared. That caring is what we carried into the next step.

By the end of Empathize we had a research repository, a wall, a deck, a set of themes, and a shared language. We did not have screens yet, and that was a sign of health. We presented the research to stakeholders in a working session rather than a performance. We asked them what surprised them. We asked them what they already knew. We asked them to help us poke holes. They were engaged. They were grateful. They said things like "this is why we brought you in." That is a good sentence to hear. We wrote it down too.

Define

Define is where empathy becomes a problem statement, and a problem statement is a gift you give the rest of the project. Without one you are just making things. With one you are making things in a direction. We spent more time on this than people usually want to spend, because getting the words right is the work. We drafted, we read out loud, we crossed out adjectives, we put them back. The version we landed on was simple on purpose: users wanted a better experience, and we were going to give them one that felt seamless, intuitive, and worthy of their trust.

I know that sounds broad. It is broad. Broad is useful at this stage because it keeps you from over-fitting to a single quote. We did write a more official "how might we" as well. How might we help people feel confident as they move through this experience. How might we reduce the moments of friction that make someone second-guess themselves. How might we create a sense of synergy between what the product says it is and what it actually feels like to use. We put those on a slide with a lot of whitespace. Whitespace makes statements feel important.

We created a persona so the problem would have a face. Her name is Sarah. Sarah is 28. She loves yoga and hates bad UX. She is busy in a way that is not dramatic, just true. She wants to do a good job. She wants to get home at a reasonable hour. She will give a product about thirty seconds of goodwill before she decides whether it is for her. She is not "not tech savvy" — I don't love that phrase — she is simply unwilling to be punished for not having memorized someone else's information architecture. We described her apartment (plants, a yoga mat that is actually used, a laptop with stickers) and her goals (feel in control, not look stupid, maybe get a little delight) and her frustrations (too many clicks, unclear next steps, things that look pretty but don't help). Sarah became the person we designed for. When we got stuck we asked what Sarah would want.

We also built a user journey map, which I am not going to over-explain because the artifact is the explanation. It had phases along the top and rows for actions, thoughts, feelings, opportunities. It was long. It was colorful. It lived in Miro and then it lived on a wall and then it lived in the appendix of every deck we made after that. People loved looking at it. It made the work feel real. If you have ever been in a meeting where someone says "where are we on the journey," you understand the value.

Scope is part of Define whether you want it to be or not. We talked about what was in and what was out. We talked about what "better" would look like if we got it right. We did not get into metrics at this stage because we wanted to stay close to the human story. Metrics can come later, once there is something in the world to measure. For now we had Sarah, we had a statement, we had a map, and we had a room full of people who were nodding. That was enough to unlock Ideate.

I should say we also defined principles. Principles are like a personality for the product. Ours were: be clear, be kind, be quick, be a partner. We printed them. We referred to them. When a idea showed up that was clever but not kind, we could point at the wall and say "is this kind." That is a useful question. It saved us from ourselves more than once, or at least it felt like it did, which is almost the same thing.

There is a temptation in Define to keep defining forever because it feels safe. Words are cheaper than screens and nobody can tell you your problem statement is buggy. We gave ourselves a deadline anyway. On a Thursday we said, this is the statement, this is Sarah, this is the map, we are moving. The team needed that. I needed that. Projects need a moment where you stop sharpening the pencil and actually draw.

Ideate

Ideate is the part people think of when they think of design, and I love it, I do, but I also think it only works if Empathize and Define were honest. We had been honest, so we let ourselves be messy in the way the process asks you to be messy, which is to say not that messy. We timeboxed. We used prompts. We filled pages. We stuck with the "quantity over quality" rule until we had more ideas than we needed, and then we started to sort.

We opened with a warmup because you cannot sprint from a standing desk straight into genius. Thirty circles on a page, fill them with anything. Then crazy eights. Then a round where you take someone else's worst idea and make it better. Then a round where you take the better version and make it worse on purpose, which sounds like a joke but actually loosens the group up. People laughed. Laughter is a design tool. It means the room is safe enough to be wrong.

The ideas themselves covered a lot of ground. A simpler first screen. A more guided first screen. A dashboard. Not a dashboard. More color. Less color. Illustration. No illustration. A wizard. Not a wizard. We wrote them all down without judging, and then we judged them, because that is the second half of ideation even if the textbooks pretend it isn't. We used dot voting. We used a two-by-two. We used "would Sarah get this in ten seconds." The ideas that survived felt obvious in retrospect, which is how you know they are good. Or at least how you know they are comfortable, and comfortable is a kind of good when you are trying to make something intuitive.

We storyboarded the winning concepts in very rough boxes. Not because the boxes were the design but because boxes force you to pick an order. First the person arrives. Then they understand where they are. Then they do the thing. Then they know they did the thing. That sequence is so classic it is almost embarrassing to write down, and yet so many products skip the "know they did the thing" part and wonder why people are anxious. We did not skip it. We put a confirmation in the storyboard and everyone nodded. Synergy.

One afternoon we tried a "negative brainstorm" where we listed ways to make the experience worse. Hide the primary action. Use jargon. Ask for the same information twice. Change the layout between steps. It was funny and then it wasn't, because a few of those anti-patterns were sitting in the current product already. That was useful. It gave us a list of things we would simply not do. Design is sometimes just a list of refusals.

We closed Ideate with a concept critique that was structured so nobody would get their feelings hurt. I like, I wish, what if. We went around the table. We captured everything. We updated the storyboard. We were aligned enough to prototype, which is all Ideate owes you. It does not owe you the answer. It owes you a promising direction and a team that can still look each other in the eye. We had both.

I want to be honest that this phase felt smooth. Sometimes you read case studies and they invent a dark night of the soul because it makes a better narrative. We did not have one. The ideas came, the dots clustered, the storyboard made sense, we moved. That is allowed. When the process works it can look like it was always going to work. I think that is a feature, not a bug. Trust the process and the process will carry you. We trusted it.

Prototype

We prototyped. We started in low fidelity because that is what you do, and then we moved to mid, and then we moved to high, and each jump felt earned. Low fidelity was boxes and labels. Mid fidelity was boxes and labels and a little bit of hierarchy. High fidelity was the real type, the real color, the real components. By the time we were in high fidelity the screens looked like a product, which is a dangerous moment because people stop thinking and start decorating. We tried to keep thinking.

The main flow we built was the happy path, start to finish, because if the happy path is not happy you do not have a product, you have a museum of states. We designed the arrival. We designed the orientation. We designed the action. We designed the confirmation. We designed an empty state because empty states are where personality shows. We designed an error state because errors are where trust is won or thrown away. We did not design every possible edge because time is real, but we designed enough that a clickable prototype could be walked without falling through a hole.

Components saved us. We made buttons and inputs and cards and navigational pieces and we reused them so the screens would feel like they belonged to the same family. Consistency is a kind of kindness. Sarah should not have to relearn a button because someone got bored. We had a small set of colors, a small set of type sizes, a small set of radii. Small sets are easier to keep in your head. They also photograph well in a case study, although I am trying not to think about the case study while I am in the file. That way lies fussiness.

We named layers. We named frames. We named the file. I care about this more than is normal. A prototype is a communication tool, not just a preview, and if another person cannot find the checkout flow without a guided tour, the prototype has failed even if it looks beautiful. We left notes in the file. We left a cover page. We left a tiny legend. These are not glamorous artifacts. They are how you don't hate yourself in week six.

Interactions were kept simple. We used the standard transitions. We did not invent a motion language, we borrowed one that already felt familiar. Familiar is intuitive. Intuitive is the assignment. There was a version of me earlier in my career that would have put a bounce on everything and called it delight. Delight is not bounce. Delight is the relief of things working the way you quietly hoped they would. We aimed for that.

Reviews in this phase were frequent and short. We did not wait for a big reveal. Big reveals make people perform. We showed work while it was still ugly enough to criticize. Then we showed it again when it was prettier. The prettier version got fewer comments, which always happens, so we had to remember the comments from the ugly version and keep them alive. That is a discipline. Pretty is a solvent. It dissolves critique. You have to write the critique down before the gradients show up.

I keep saying "we built screens" and not saying what was on them, and that is somewhat on purpose. The screens are a vessel for the journey we already mapped. If I walk you through every module I will put you to sleep and I will also pretend that the modules were the point. The point was a person moving through a thing without getting hurt. The modules served that. We had a header that told you where you were. We had a primary action that told you what to do. We had supporting text that told you why it was safe. We had a way back. That is the whole product, over and over, until it is done.

There was a desktop version and there was a version that was more compact. We did both because people use both. We did not treat the compact one as an afterthought, or at least we tried not to. We stacked the same ideas. We kept the same language. We made sure the primary action was still the primary action when your thumb was doing the work. Thumb reach came up. We talked about it. We moved a button. We moved it back. We left it in the place that looked right in the mockup, which is sometimes how these things go, and then we told ourselves we would watch it in testing.

Test

Testing is a gift you give your future self. We scheduled sessions, we recruited, we wrote a script, we resisted the urge to explain the prototype while people were using it. That last part is hard. You want to help. Helping contaminates the data. We sat on our hands. We said "what do you think that does" until it felt rude, and then we said it one more time.

People got through the flow. That was the headline. They clicked the things we wanted them to click, not always in the order we imagined, but they arrived. They said it seemed straightforward. They said it looked clean. They said they could see themselves using it. We asked them to think aloud and they did, in that slightly performative way people do when they know they are being good research participants. We still took every word seriously. "I like this" is a finding. "I'm not sure" is a finding. "Where do I go next" is a finding even if they figure it out two seconds later.

We asked them about Sarah, in a way. Not by name. We asked whether this felt like it was made for someone like them. Most people said yes, or said a version of yes that we decided to hear as yes. That alignment between the persona and the participants felt like a closed loop. Research in, design out, research in again. The snake eats its tail in a healthy way. We updated a few labels after the sessions. We increased the contrast on a button that one person squinted at. We called that iteration. Then we tested those changes in the sense that we looked at them internally and agreed they were better. Perfect is the enemy of done, and we were approaching done.

There is a version of this section that would include charts and success rates and a highlighter over a quote that changes everything. We did not have a quote that changed everything. We had a general sense that we were on the right track, and we had a client who was ready to see the polished file. We synthesized the sessions into a handful of slides. Green, yellow, a little bit of red that we had already addressed. We recommended moving forward. Everyone was ready to move forward. Testing had done its job, which was to give the project a blessing.

We also sent a follow-up survey to the people who had sat with us, mostly to say thank you and partly to see if anything would surface once they were back at their desks. A few people replied. They were kind. They said they enjoyed it. They said good luck. We added those notes to the repository. The repository at this point was a creature with many limbs: interview notes, survey tabs, journey map, persona, principles, prototype links, test scripts, test notes, decks. I am not going to pretend I will open all of it again. But it exists, and existing is a form of rigor.

After testing we did a final visual pass. This is not official in the five steps but everybody does it. Kerning, alignment, the last 5%. We exported. We named the exports like adults. We recorded a walkthrough video in case someone wanted to click without clicking. We put a bow on the file. Then we presented.

Outcome

The client loved it! They said we had really listened. They said the experience felt like a step change even though, if I am being careful with language, I cannot tell you what changed in the business because that was never the assignment we wrote down. The assignment was a better experience. We delivered a better experience. You could see it in the room. People smiled at the screens. Someone said "this is so much more intuitive." Someone else said "Sarah would like this," which was a joke and also the highest compliment available to us.

We handed over the files, the prototype, the principles, the persona, the journey map, and a short guide to how we hoped the system would be used going forward. We offered to stay involved. There was a warm ending. There were virtual high fives. There was the particular quiet that happens after a presentation when you realize you can check Slack without adrenaline. That quiet is my favorite part of the job and I never put it in the deck.

I do not have a before-and-after metric to put in a big number here. I have a story about a team that followed the steps, trusted the user, and made something that felt better than what was there before. I think that story is enough. If you have ever shipped anything you know that the real outcome is often just this: it exists now, and people are not mad, and some of them are even excited. We got that. I'll take it.

What I learned

I learned the importance of empathy. I learned that the process exists for a reason, and that when you skip a step you can feel it, so we didn't skip any. I learned that synergy is not just a word, it is what happens when research and design and a stakeholder group are actually listening to each other. I learned that Sarah is everywhere — that there is always a 28-year-old who loves yoga and hates bad UX, waiting for someone to take her seriously. I learned that I still love this work, even on the days that are mostly documentation.

I learned that writing it all down takes longer than making the screens, and that maybe that is okay, because the thinking is the design too. If you have read this far, thank you. I wanted you to have the whole picture, not just the pretty parts. Project Phoenix taught me so much, and I am proud of what we made together. Onward.

If Project Phoenix sounds like the kind of partnership you want, you know where to find me. Let's keep putting people in the middle of the process and see what happens. I have a pretty good guess, but I am willing to be surprised. That, I think, is the whole job.
